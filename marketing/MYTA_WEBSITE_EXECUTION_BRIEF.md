# MyTA Website Execution Brief

**Status:** Approved source of truth for website strategy and visual design  
**Version:** July 2026  
**Use:** Read before creating visual references, design targets, or Codex implementation tasks

## 1. The website’s single job

The website should create qualified conversations with university instructors who may be a fit for a focused MyTA pilot.

Its secondary job is to give instructional design, academic leadership, and university technology teams enough confidence to continue the conversation.

The website is not currently responsible for closing a campus-wide purchase, explaining every future feature, proving completed compliance work, or presenting the complete MyTA roadmap.

A successful visitor should be able to answer four questions quickly:

1. What is MyTA?
2. What problem does it solve for me?
3. Why is it different from the tools I already know?
4. What should I do next?

## 2. Primary customer

### Primary audience

A university instructor evaluating whether MyTA could help in one course.

The homepage should make the instructor feel:

1. This is a real assignment and learning problem, not a generic AI pitch.
2. I decide what the AI uses and how it may help.
3. My students still have to think and produce the work.
4. The product could show me something useful before the next major assessment.
5. Trying it can begin with one assignment or one course.
6. I am not being asked to replace my LMS or commit my institution to a large rollout.

### Secondary audience

Instructional designers, teaching and learning staff, department leaders, deans, and academic leaders.

They should understand:

1. The product has a clear pedagogical purpose.
2. The student remains cognitively active.
3. MyTA is focused on the assignment workflow.
4. A bounded pilot can generate evidence before broader adoption.
5. The product is not being marketed as a universal solution for every course.

### Approval audience

University technology, privacy, security, accessibility, legal, and procurement teams.

They should understand:

1. MyTA is intended to complement existing LMS workflows.
2. The company is not overstating technical maturity.
3. Current and planned capabilities are clearly separated.
4. Privacy, accessibility, security, permissions, and data boundaries are part of the adoption process.
5. The first step is a focused pilot conversation, not an institution-wide deployment.

## 3. Core customer problem

The website should not lead with cheating detection.

The stronger problem is:

**A polished final submission no longer tells an instructor enough about whether the intended learning happened.**

In a traditional homework workflow, the instructor sees:

1. The assignment that was given.
2. The final response that was submitted.

The instructor sees much less of the work between those points:

1. Where the student became confused.
2. What the student attempted.
3. What help the student requested.
4. How AI was used.
5. What the student checked or revised.
6. Which concepts still need support.
7. Whether a polished answer reflects real understanding.

The problem is not simply that students use AI.

The problem is that instructor intent, student reasoning, AI support, and instructor follow-up are disconnected.

## 4. Public product definition

### Primary public definition

**MyTA lets instructors set the course context and rules for AI support. Students get guided help while they work, and instructors get a clearer view of where understanding breaks down.**

### Internal category

**An assignment-centered AI learning and visibility layer for higher education.**

Use the internal category for strategy and internal documents. Do not rely on it as the first sentence visitors see because it is more abstract than the customer problem.

### What MyTA is not

MyTA is not:

1. A general-purpose chatbot.
2. An AI detector.
3. A surveillance product.
4. A finished LMS replacement.
5. A publisher courseware platform.
6. An automated substitute for instructor judgment.
7. A promise that AI will always be accurate.
8. A complete solution for every course and every teaching method.

## 5. The product story

The product must always be explained as one connected assignment loop.

### Step 1: Set the assignment context

The instructor defines or approves:

1. Course materials.
2. Learning objectives.
3. Assignment expectations.
4. Approved references.
5. AI support rules.
6. Checks for understanding.

### Step 2: Guide the work

Students complete the assignment with support intended to keep them active.

The desired student behaviors are:

1. Attempt.
2. Explain.
3. Ask for help.
4. Check assumptions.
5. Examine mistakes.
6. Revise incomplete approaches.
7. Produce the final response themselves.

### Step 3: See the learning signals

MyTA is being built to surface concise signals such as:

1. Recurring misconceptions.
2. Difficult concepts or questions.
3. Common help requests.
4. Patterns in attempts and revision.
5. Areas where an individual student or the class may need support.

The website should not describe this as perfect measurement or complete visibility into learning.

### Step 4: Support instructor action

The value is not another dashboard.

The value is helping the instructor decide what to do next.

Possible actions include:

1. Revisit a difficult concept.
2. Provide targeted practice.
3. Recommend office hours.
4. Direct TA or peer support.
5. Address a recurring misconception.
6. Adjust a future assignment.

## 6. Customer-centered value proposition

### For instructors

MyTA is intended to help instructors:

1. Decide how AI may support an assignment.
2. Keep course materials and learning goals inside the experience.
3. Give students useful help without producing the final response for them.
4. See where students or the class are getting stuck.
5. Turn learning signals into more targeted support.
6. Explore the workflow through a bounded course pilot.

### For students

MyTA is intended to help students:

1. Get support within the context of the course.
2. Work through difficult material without immediately receiving the finished answer.
3. Explain, check, and revise their thinking.
4. Identify what they still need to understand.
5. Receive more relevant follow-up support.

### For universities

MyTA offers a focused way to evaluate instructor-directed AI in a real course workflow.

A pilot can help the institution evaluate:

1. Teaching value.
2. Student experience.
3. Workflow fit.
4. Privacy and data requirements.
5. Accessibility requirements.
6. Security and technology review needs.
7. Whether broader adoption would be justified.

## 7. Defensible differentiation

Do not claim that other products lack tutoring, course context, assignments, analytics, feedback, or instructor controls.

The difference is what each product is primarily organized around.

### Publisher ecosystems

Publisher AI generally begins with publisher-authored content, courseware, question banks, assessments, and publisher workflows.

### LMS AI

LMS AI generally begins with broad platform needs such as course creation, administration, content, assessment, analytics, accessibility, and institutional workflows.

### Standalone tutors and study assistants

Standalone tutors generally begin with the student’s question, explanation, practice, or study need.

### MyTA

**MyTA begins with the instructor’s assignment and is being built to connect instructor intent, guided student work, learning signals, and instructor action.**

### Four positioning pillars

1. **Your course context**  
   The instructor’s materials, goals, expectations, and approved references shape the experience.

2. **Your rules for support**  
   The instructor decides where AI can help and where the student must work independently.

3. **Active student thinking**  
   The experience is intended to prompt attempts, explanations, checks, and revision.

4. **Useful next steps**  
   Learning signals are organized around decisions an instructor can make.

## 8. Tone and language

The website should sound like it understands the instructor’s course, workload, judgment, and responsibility.

### Use

1. You and your course.
2. Assignment.
3. Course context.
4. Learning goals.
5. Guided support.
6. Student reasoning.
7. Checks for understanding.
8. Where students are getting stuck.
9. What to do next.
10. One assignment or one course.
11. Review the fit before scaling.

### Avoid

1. Revolutionize.
2. Transform education.
3. Empower.
4. Seamless.
5. Next generation.
6. Cutting edge.
7. Game changing.
8. Unlock.
9. Supercharge.
10. Complete solution.
11. AI-powered everything.
12. Generic personalized learning claims.
13. Fear-based claims about cheating.
14. Language that makes instructors sound resistant or behind.
15. Language that implies students cannot be trusted.
16. Corporate language that hides the actual product.

### Voice principles

1. Lead with the instructor’s decision or problem.
2. Use direct second-person language where it improves clarity.
3. Describe the product through the assignment workflow.
4. Explain benefits through practical teaching actions.
5. Make institutional trust language calm and precise.
6. State limitations honestly.
7. Do not make every heading a slogan.
8. Do not use an em dash in public website copy.

## 9. Lessons from relevant product websites

The MyTA message should apply the following patterns without copying another company’s brand.

### Class Companion

Use the direct teacher benefit, visible product workflow, and simple sequence from assignment creation to student support to instructor insight.

### Gradescope

Reduce change anxiety with language that makes the product feel compatible with existing assignments and teaching practice.

### Brisk

Address tool overload through clear workflow-fit language. Explain where the product fits instead of describing it as another destination.

### Canvas IgniteAI

Use the customer language of alignment with how instructors teach, control over how AI is used, and visibility into the experience.

### Turnitin Clarity

Use the clear assignment-level framing, instructor-controlled AI support, guidance rather than answer production, and visibility into the work behind the final submission.

### FeedbackFruits

Lead with a recognizable teaching problem and explain the value through active learning and practical instructor action rather than abstract technology claims.

## 10. Homepage jobs

The homepage should contain five main jobs.

### 1. Hero

Explain what MyTA is, show the real product, and offer the primary actions.

### 2. Problem

Explain why the final submission no longer tells the full story.

### 3. How MyTA works

Show the assignment loop through real product screenshots.

### 4. Why MyTA and how it fits

Explain the assignment-centered difference, instructor control, LMS companion strategy, and focused institutional review path.

### 5. Pilot

Make one assignment or one course feel like a practical next step.

Detailed learning research, competitor analysis, and institutional review information should use progressive disclosure or move to later pages.

## 11. Conversion strategy

### Primary conversion

A qualified instructor requests a pilot conversation.

### Secondary conversion

A visitor watches the product demo.

### Primary call to action

**Request a Pilot**

### Secondary call to action

**Watch the Demo**

### Recommended pilot path

1. Short conversation.
2. Tailored walkthrough using one course or assignment.
3. Discussion of learning goals and support boundaries.
4. Review of technology, privacy, security, or accessibility requirements.
5. Small pilot decision.

The website should not promise that every conversation will become a pilot.

## 12. Public claims policy

### Safe language

Use:

1. Designed to.
2. Intended to.
3. Being built to.
4. Structured around.
5. Instructor-approved course context.
6. Guided support.
7. Learning signals.
8. Designed to work alongside LMS workflows.
9. Informed by established learning principles.
10. A focused course pilot.

### Claims requiring verification before publication

Do not publish without current evidence:

1. FERPA compliant.
2. SOC 2 compliant or certified.
3. WCAG compliant or certified.
4. Official LMS integration.
5. Single sign-on.
6. Roster synchronization.
7. Grade return.
8. Specific data retention terms.
9. Guaranteed learning gains.
10. Proven reductions in cheating.
11. Perfect AI accuracy.
12. Verified customer counts.
13. Testimonials.
14. Institutional partnerships.
15. Pilot outcomes.
16. Pricing beyond the currently approved pilot offer.

## 13. Message acceptance test

Before approving any design or copy, test it across four layers.

### Clarity

Can an instructor explain what MyTA does after ten seconds?

### Relevance

Does the instructor immediately recognize the assignment and learning problem?

### Value

Is it clear what the instructor and student gain?

### Differentiation

Is it clear why MyTA is not simply an open chatbot, publisher tool, or general LMS feature?

The message fails when a visitor concludes that MyTA is mainly an AI detector, a broad LMS, a generic tutor, or a surveillance product.

## 14. Locked strategic statement

**MyTA should be marketed as an instructor-controlled assignment learning loop. The instructor sets the context, the student does the thinking, useful learning signals return to the instructor, and the first step can be one focused course pilot.**
